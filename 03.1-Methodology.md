# III) Methodology 
In this section, we will explain the methodology of this thesis in detail. To do this, we examine the specific case of labor law in Quebec and the issues related to modeling in this context. In this section, we explain the reasoning behind the choice of this specific legal field and provide a detailed breakdown of how Quebec legal types function. Since there are no ready-to-use databases available for this work, the second part details our data collection method, explaining the potential biases and challenges it may involve. Finally, we propose to formalize the task we wish to accomplish by explaining the mathematical and computational methods used to solve it.
## III.1) Quebec Labour law case
Western law is divided into two major legal traditions: civil law and common law. While we will not describe all the detailed components of these systems, we must explain the principal difference, which lies in the way laws are created and applied. Civil law relies on codified statutes enacted by the legislature. It is important to note that courts are not bound to one another by their decisions. Thus, while precedents are important for argumentation, they do not constitute a binding order to act in a given way for the adjudicating body. In common law, courts create the law through their decisions and are bound by them. Consequently, to ensure consistency in decision-making, judges must follow the rulings of higher courts. In Quebec, our system is a hybrid one, drawing on elements of both civil law and common law depending on the jurisdiction. However, common law culture has infused judicial practice, and precedents carry crucial weight in judges' decision-making. In this context, searching for and identifying relevant cases for pleading constitutes an essential task in case preparation. The objective of this task is to cite judgments—and the pertinent passages within them—to persuade judges to act consistently with their past rulings. While lawyers today use search engines like CanLII or SOQUIJ to find cases using keywords, refining the selection of the most relevant cases often relies on the acquired knowledge of legal professionals. The core issue lies in the time required to identify the most significant judgments needed to build a persuasive argument. Labor law in Quebec, on the other hand, falls under administrative law. This area of law covers all the rules governing relations between the administrés (citizens, in this case employers and employees) and the public administration involved. The administrative body responsible for remedies and rights regarding occupational health and safety is the Commission des normes, de l'équité, de la santé et de la sécurité du travail (CNESST). Its primary role is to govern relations between employers and their employees to ensure that standards are met. In the event of a dispute or challenge, the Tribunal administratif du travail (TAT) is the specialized administrative tribunal for these matters. To ensure fair representation of the parties' interests, the TAT relies on a body of laws, regulations, and precedents to rule on the challenges brought before it. It is essential for the parties to rely on these elements to substantiate and defend their interests and maximize their chances of success before administrative judges. It is worth noting that parties always have the option to appeal these decisions before higher courts following a specific process. However, the TAT remains the tribunal of first instance in Quebec for disputes involving occupational health and safety. This brief description of the Quebec legal framework highlights the importance of the casematching task for building pleadings and conducting strategic intelligence for the parties. Although analyzing legal cases using NLP emerged as the central theme during the development of this thesis, we quickly realized that the pool of available judgments was vast. Indeed, many fields of law exist, each with its own underlying logic and specific vocabulary. Consequently, building our corpus on a wide variety of legal domains could lead to issues stemming from this broad domain diversity. To simplify our work, we decided to focus on labor law, and more specifically on workplace accidents and occupational diseases, for several reasons: 
* 1. Business Intelligence Relevance: The first reason relates to the significance of these cases for businesses. As this thesis is conducted within a Business Intelligence program, it is essential that our research focus aligns with business challenges faced by organizations. Occupational health and safety is a domain that directly affects companies and workers, often with long-term impacts. Offering better intelligence on these cases serves not only to build better pleading tools, but also to provide new data to act proactively in the sector, ensuring stronger strategic monitoring on the subject. These elements can yield both economic gains and well-being improvements within companies.
* 2.  Resource Constraints: The second reason lies in our limited available resources. Having to collect our data independently made it impossible to process hundreds of thousands of texts to build our corpus. Because judgments are lengthy documents, the computational resources required for data processing can quickly increase exponentially. Refocusing our work on a specific sector allows us to limit the dimensionality of our dataset, facilitating the use of algorithms.
* 3. Vocabulary Homogeneity: The third reason concerns the linguistic homogeneity of our corpus. Choosing this domain ensures a high degree of case homogeneity, allowing us to better grasp the tribunal's decision-making logic. Furthermore, the framework for occupational diseases is well defined by the Act respecting industrial accidents and occupational diseases (AIAOD / LATMP), which facilitates the interpretation of our results.
* 4. Data Centralization: Finally, having a single body centralize challenges to CNESST decisions—the TAT—facilitates data retrieval. Indeed, under sections 4 and 6 of the Act respecting the Administrative Labour Tribunal (LITAT), the TAT holds exclusive jurisdiction over challenges regarding occupational diseases against CNESST decisions. Although appeals to higher courts remain possible, we can consider that the vast majority of cases relating to these topics are heard by this tribunal. This simplifies the construction of our dataset for analysis by allowing us to focus solely on decisions issued by this court. While these choices were made to streamline our work, they also involve limitations related to the restricted sample size over time, which are discussed later in our section on limitations and future work. Having established these elements specific to our subject of analysis, we now proceed with data collection to build our study corpus. The next section details our approach to constructing the dataset.
## III.2) Data collection 
As described previously, we decided to focus on challenges in occupational disease cases at the TAT. Two major websites provide access to public Canadian judgment data: CanLII and SOQUIJ. Both platforms offer search engines based on keyword queries to search for jurisprudence within their databases. In addition, these search engines feature filtering tools allowing users to select jurisdictions, date ranges, case numbers, and more. We opted for SOQUIJ due to our prior experience with the platform and the facilitated access provided via the HEC Montréal institutional portal. Using the query "maladie professionnelle" (occupational disease), we obtained over 200,000 judgments across all jurisdictions and more than 100,000 for the labor tribunal alone. To reduce this figure, we decided to apply filters to our search. Our choice of filter focused on a date range from which to sample our cases. For practical considerations, we chose a timeframe prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. Indeed, this period could introduce bias into the data; for instance, the pace of hearings was slowed due to various lockdown and social distancing measures. More generally, to avoid any bias associated with this unique period, we selected a search timeframe prior to 2020. For the sake of consistency, we required these data to span a full calendar year of judgments. Consequently, we searched for data published between January 1 and December 31 of a given study year. With this set of filters, our goal was to select a year containing sufficient data for the models we deploy to be robust. Nevertheless, we wished to avoid a year with an excessively large volume of data, as legal text consists of long-form documents compared to the shorter text formats commonly used in NLP (such as forum comments). Finally, we still aimed to collect relatively recent data to work with decisions that align as closely as possible with the current state of the law. As a result, we decided not to consider data prior to 2013. Taking all these constraints into account, we selected the year 2017, which comprises 5,403 judgments related to occupational diseases within the Administrative Labour Tribunal's Occupational Health and Safety Division. We then proceeded to download the data in PDF format for our study. Following the data download, we had to process the format, as PDFs cannot be directly ingested into Python. We developed a script using the Py2PDF package to convert them into .txt files. These manipulations presented challenges related to document layout, which does not always transcribe accurately into plain text format—a problem that primarily arose in documents containing tables. We isolated these cases to process them separately. Finally, we classified them according to the tribunal's naming convention, which follows the format 2017QCTAT1 (representing the case year, the jurisdiction code, and the sequential case number). While this method allowed us to obtain usable data, we must acknowledge that we relied on a search engine algorithm to retrieve them. This implies that we are working with data that has already been filtered by a proprietary model. Furthermore, we do not know the underlying details of this search algorithm, which introduces potential selection bias. However, our study does not focus on identifying cases that fall under the scope of occupational diseases, but rather on calculating document proximity within this domain. Aside from this, in the absence of a superior data collection method, we utilized the most suitable tools available to us. Thus, while we highlight this factor, its impact on our research remains minimal, though its existence must be noted. 
## III.3) Formalization of the Task
### III.3.a) Semantic dimension :
Semantics is the study of linguistic meaning in language expression. This branch of linguistics focuses on the construction of sentences and ideas through words. To this end, Ferdinand de Saussure proposes studying linguistic signs as an interconnected system that conveys the meaning of the concepts evoked by the author (Benveniste, 1964).In our case, this enables us to examine the meanings embedded in our texts and the underlying themes addressed by analyzing the words contained within them. Based on this theoretical foundation, several approaches can be considered for semantic text modeling. The first approach postulates that a document carries an overall meaning, and its constituent words are the signs that define this meaning; in other words, every document conveys a distinct linguistic meaning. Under this assumption, words within corpus documents can be represented as word vectors, followed by the application of unsupervised clustering algorithms—such as K-means or DBSCAN—to group texts together based on their similarities. In the vast majority of cases, these algorithms require specifying the number of clusters a priori or defining specific hyperparameters, such as the neighborhood radius ($\epsilon$) in DBSCAN. The second approach postulates that because topics are concepts, they are represented by word co-occurrences and associations. Consequently, a single document can cover multiple topics and carry several meanings. Within this framework, documents are modeled as word vectors, and the probability of each document belonging to a given topic is estimated. Furthermore, the Dirichlet distribution allows us to control the shape of topic distributions via an alpha hyperparameter ($\alpha$). This aligns with the linguistic premise that while multiple topics may be raised within a text, a single predominant topic often dominates. For this form of modeling, the most widely used algorithm is Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), which incorporates all these constraints to simultaneously model topics through word distributions and documents through their topic probabilities. In this thesis, we chose to utilize topic modeling to measure the semantic dimension of our corpus. This decision was driven by several factors regarding the respective strengths and limitations of these approaches, detailed below. First, legal documents are relatively lengthy. In our corpus, the shortest text contains 987 words, whereas the longest exceeds 120,000 words. Given this observation, we hypothesize that multiple topics are likely addressed within individual texts. Moreover, modeling topics through word vectors enhances interpretability compared to document clustering, where understanding the underlying rationale for cluster assignments requires deeper manual analysis. Second, our corpus consists of 5,403 texts. Consequently, our prior knowledge of the corpus content is limited, making it particularly challenging to determine the appropriate number of clusters without prior intuition regarding the underlying classes. Third, we accounted for future extensions of this work that would incorporate new data. As established previously, a vast volume of court judgments is available for analysis; thus, anticipating the scalability of future datasets—which can rapidly grow in size—is essential. Several variants of LDA offer incremental training methods to continuously integrate new data into the model (Uray et al., 2007; Wenchao et al., 2022; Gerasimenko et al., 2023). In summary, LDA’s potential scalability, interpretability, and suitability for our data motivated our selection of this model to extract corpus semantics. Finally, we must highlight the existence of alternative topic modeling algorithms grounded in recent deep learning advances, such as BERTopic or, more recently, TopicGPT. However, these models appear to underperform LDA on long-form texts. Additionally, fine-tuning their performance via text preprocessing presents greater challenges due to their sensitivity to word removal. As explained above, LDA analyzes the topics of a corpus by examining the word vectors that constitute these topics, and then assigns a probability of belonging to one or more topics to each text. ### III.3.b) The Legal Dimension 
While the semantic aspect is an important dimension for grouping topics, it does not necessarily capture the nature of the legal arguments put forward by the parties to defend their cases. Indeed, legal pleadings may address several distinct themes while relying on similar legal arguments, and conversely, similar topics may employ radically different legal rhetorics. For instance, two cases might both concern a knee fracture, yet on the legal level, one may deal with the apportionment of compensation while the other focuses on the recognition of an occupational disease. Matching these two cases solely on semantic grounds is insufficient for a legal practitioner whose primary goal is to build a coherent argument before the court. Our reflection thus turned toward the nature of common law and the importance of precedents in judicial decision-making. As previously explained, courts in the Quebec justice system are bound by past decisions through the principle of precedent. Consequently, the vast majority of judgments cite prior court decisions to substantiate their rulings. Judgments can therefore be viewed as a vast, interconnected network that shapes the interpretation of the law in Quebec and Canada. Furthermore, statutory provisions form the baseline of legal procedure for the courts. Much like case law, judges cite statutes and specific statutory provisions within their decisions to support the grounds for their rulings. Thus, statutory provisions can also be integrated into the network of texts that constitute the Canadian legal pyramid. What makes this task more tractable is the overall standardization of citation formats compiled within legal texts. Extracting these references is therefore straightforward, and we will describe this extraction process in greater detail in the results section. This modeling framework allows us to create a directed graph representation where the nodes represent our corpus texts, statutes, and cited case law. Modeling the edges between these nodes posed a key challenge in this work. To address it, we had to consider two primary elements: The first concerns the modeling of statutes. For example, the most frequently encountered statute in our dataset is the Act respecting industrial accidents and occupational diseases. While judges frequently reference specific statutory sections, in certain cases they cite the statute as a whole. Early in our research, we created distinct edges between texts, statutory chapters, and entire statutes. However, the problem with this approach lies in the calculation of distance and similarity metrics: because the objects are distinct, a text citing a specific section would share no overlap with a text citing the entire statute. Seeking to preserve a fine level of granularity, we drew inspiration from prior work by Shounak et al. (2021). In their paper, the authors employ a hierarchical representation of statutes. Following this approach, we decided to model statutory texts hierarchically, as illustrated in Figure 3 below, which we produced.

Indeed, statutory sections are connected to the statutory chapters from which they derive. Furthermore, this implies that texts citing a section, by definition, cite the statute to which the chapter belongs. Although we considered representing our graph with undirected edges, it seemed more logical to respect the direction of references. Specifically, since the corpus texts cite references, this implies a directed edge in that direction. This logic is crucial because we cannot assume that case law references—which by definition predate the judgments in the corpus—rely on the latter. Therefore, it would be incorrect to treat citations as a bilateral relationship, especially if we consider expanding our graph over multiple years in the future. ## III.4) Extraction of Citation Data and Graph Construction 
In addition to the quantitative semantic analyses, we conducted an investigation of the foundational elements of our graph network: citations to court decisions and statutes within our corpus texts. To explore the network, we constructed the graph by extracting citations using regular expressions (regex). Regular expressions are sequences of characters that define a search pattern to match within text. The advantage inherent to legal texts lies in the standardization of citation formats maintained by legal publishing standards. Consequently, citation conventions can be comprehensively identified using dedicated reference guides (Luelles & Ringuette, 2019). For statutes, we replaced all cited statutory names with their official publication numbers, such as: An Act respecting insurance: CQLR, c. A-32. Once completed, we sought a method to extract the specific sections associated with these statutory texts. We explored several options to achieve this task. One considered option was leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to extract this information. After several trials with ChatGPT and Gemini, we observed that these models frequently omit citations in their output. Another avenue we explored was training a supervised model to extract this information from text. However, this approach requires a substantial volume of annotated textual data for training, which was not available to us. For these reasons, we opted for the following method: we tokenized the sentences in the corpus to extract statutory sections alongside their corresponding statutes. We then filtered the sentences to retain the term "section" (or "article") and the numeric values following it. Finally, we manually reviewed and corrected the extractions to ensure consistency. From this process, we obtained a dataset with a column containing the case identifier of the source text (e.g., QCTAT123) and a column containing the extracted statutory reference along with its associated sections. Initially, we retained this data structure, which followed the architecture shown in the first diagram of Figure 3. However, as explained in subsection III.3.b) (Legal Dimension), to maintain legal coherence regarding the hierarchy of sources for similarity calculations, we opted for the second architecture presented in Figure 3. Thus, we established edges between statutes and their respective sections, as well as between the source texts and the referenced statutes. Finally, we removed duplicate entries from the list of edges, postulating that the frequency of references to the same statute does not automatically signify its relative importance to the legal argument. Indeed, because our cases involve occupational health and safety, the Act respecting industrial accidents and occupational diseases is frequently cited as the primary governing legislation for these matters. Yet, within a single paragraph, a judge may cite it multiple times without referencing a specific section to support their point. Therefore, to avoid biasing our subsequent analyses, we chose to retain only a single citation per distinct statutory text for each source document. Regarding judicial citations (case law), the task proved more straightforward. We searched for court abbreviations cited within case docket numbers. In line with our approach for statutes, we relied on the legal citation manual (Luelles & Ringuette, 2019) and compiled all abbreviations cataloged in that work. This allowed us to focus our extraction script specifically on the court abbreviations present within our corpus. Based on this list, we examined the distinct docket number patterns for each abbreviation to craft regular expressions capable of matching case numbers in the corpus. For example, the decision Précibois inc. et Brisebois is cited under the reference C.L.P. 111268-62A-9902. This citation format is standardized across all decisions of the Commission des lésions professionnelles, with only the numerical identifiers varying. Following this step, we conducted a manual audit of the extracted case law citations and corrected specific edge cases. Consistent with our decision to deduplicate the edge table for statutes, we applied the same logic to remove duplicates here. Next, we concatenated the two datasets to produce a unified edge table for the graph. Finally, we built a table of unique graph nodes, compiling and categorizing references to statutes, corpus texts, and cited court decisions. While the methodology implemented served the scope of this thesis effectively, we emphasize that further improvements could automate this pipeline. We discuss these aspects in our chapter on Limitations and Future Work. Following data extraction, we generated a graph visualization in Gephi to analyze the network structure alongside our computational analyses in Python using the NetworkX library.
