# IV.3) Graph network structure analysis
In our chapter on the formalization of the task regarding the legal dimension, as well as in the sub-chapter on graph data collection, we explained elements of data extraction. Once our data tables are constituted, we proceed to model the citations using a mix of Gephi and Python's NetworkX library. What interests us, before proceeding to the study of our results, is the structure of the graph and, more particularly, the potential groupings of nodes around each other. To analyze this, we rely on the modularity calculation included in Gephi, which is based on the Louvain method that we describe here. Next, we examine more closely the modularity and the groupings formed in the graph for the distinct case of jurisprudence citations, laws, and finally, for the entire graph. Then, we propose looking at the structure of the graph. For this, we divide the graph into two: the jurisprudence network and the law network. Following the analysis of the different metrics for these networks, we look at the complete network to better understand its structure before presenting the results in the following chapter.
## IV.3.a) Graph evaluation metrics
In order to analyze our graph, we rely on graphical elements, but we also look at statistical elements. In this part, we explain the main metrics we use to analyze the graph.
### IV.3.a.i) Node degree
To begin, we look at the elements related to node degrees. Degree is defined by the number of links a node has with others. There are two types of degrees, outgoing and incoming, depending on the destination of the connection. A connection leaving the node is considered outgoing, while one pointing towards the node is incoming. The value of studying degree lies in analyzing the distribution of node connections in the graph. In our case, texts are often the origin points of the edges, so we expect to see a stronger incoming degree in the graph. More generally, this analysis allows us to verify the points raised in our data exploration.
### IV.3.a.ii) Connected components
The second study we propose concerns connected components. Connected components are elements of the graph that are more or less complex and are not connected to any larger subgraph (Clark, 1991). In simpler terms, we are talking here about floating parts of the graph that are not connected to the others. For these calculations, we rely on the tools provided by Gephi, which use the "depth-first search" (DFS) algorithm. DFS is an iterative method that searches for neighboring nodes, resembling the BFS mentioned in our chapter on the legal dimension. The main differences between these two approaches are primarily related to computation optimization methods. However, the method proposed by Gephi proves to be effective in our case. Through this, we obtain the number of connected components that our graph contains as well as their degrees of connection, that is, their size and the importance of the connections they contain. This could show us isolated portions of the graph that present certain particularities. This can reveal judgments of a specific nature that we must then explain. We must still remember that a certain number of texts do not possess citations, which leads them to form isolated components that we treat in a particular way.
### IV.3.a.iii) Node communities in the graph
In a graph, subsets of more densely clustered nodes can form what is called a community. The latter generally indicate a strong interconnection between the nodes in that portion of the graph. In our case, we wish to look at the communities in the graph of the closest nodes in order to see if certain judgments or laws have a more significant connection than others in our corpus. In order to measure this, we calculate modularity in the graph to divide it into different groups, which allows us to isolate node communities. As mentioned earlier, modularity is calculated via the Louvain method using Gephi. This method is based on the work of Vincent Blondel et al., who propose an approach to calculate modularity more efficiently in large graph networks (Blondel et al., 2008). Where $Q$ is the modularity, $k_i$ and $k_j$ are the sum of the weights of the edges connected to nodes $i$ and $j$, $m$ is the number of edges in the graph, $N$ is the total number of nodes in the graph, and $c_i$ and $c_j$ are the communities to which nodes $i$ and $j$ belong.Finally, $\delta$ represents the Kronecker delta function, and, in the case of modularity, if $\delta(c_i, c_j) = 1$ then $c_i$ and $c_j$ belong to the same grouping; conversely, if this variable takes the value zero, then $c_i$ and $c_j$ are in two distinct sets. Modularity is a scale ranging from -1 to 1 that measures the feasibility of dividing the network into communities. In practice, modularity is generally positive. Thus, a modularity close to 0 indicates that the network cannot be divided into coherent community structures. Conversely, a modularity close to 1 indicates strong coherence in the network's community structures. While this metric is practical for determining the sets underlying a network, the challenge lies in the calculation methods required to obtain this result in large graphs. The Louvain algorithm addresses this issue through a two-step approach (Clark, 1991). In the first part of the algorithm, the authors postulate that each node corresponds to a community, meaning the graph is composed of as many communities as there are nodes. Following this, the gain or loss of graph modularity is evaluated by grouping neighboring nodes into a community. If this grouping results in a gain, the nodes are grouped together. The process is repeated for each node in the network until no further improvement is possible. Then, in the second phase of the algorithm, a new graph is reconstructed by taking the node communities formed in the first part as nodes. It is thanks to the synergy of these two methods that we can ensure optimal computation in large networks.

## IV.3.d) The complete citation network
Finally, we analyze the structure of the complete graph in this final section. Although in our work we detail the structure of the graph in subgraphs of jurisprudence and laws, we initially built the complete graph and then filtered the law and jurisprudence nodes in order to analyze the graphs separately. Indeed, to create our graph in the Gephi application, we had to review our extraction data. By reviewing the texts without references identified in the first graph, we noticed that a few references had not been extracted. Among these references were former tribunal classifications such as T.T., which is the former name of the TAT and was not included in our legal reference guide. These adjustments involved 30 texts from the corpus and were integrated into all of our prior analyses. This brings us to the complete graph presented in Figure 6. This leaves us with a bipartite graph containing 17,447 nodes and 65,866 edges.

These components are all composed of two nodes: a corpus reference text and an attached jurisprudential citation text. None of these components include laws, which is explained by the very narrow nature of the legal corpus generally used in judgments. Case law, on the other hand, is broad and encompasses all valid judgments published since the inception of common law. However, the scope of these citations can be narrowed, as it is widely accepted that more recent judgments carry greater weight in legal advocacy. Considering these different factors, we observe a greater depth in jurisprudence, and some of these references may be unique. In our situation, less than 0.15% of our corpus relies on precedents unique to this corpus. We can therefore minimize the impact of these components in the graph and focus on the strongest component.

This figure indicates a community structure, meaning this graph is divisible into sets of densely connected networks.
