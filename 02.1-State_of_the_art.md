# I) Literature Review 
The vast majority of data from the judicial system is textual in nature. From this observation, tools inherited from NLP naturally emerged as an obvious choice for bringing artificial intelligence instruments into the legal field. While computational approaches to law can be traced back to the early second half of the twentieth century (Kort, 1957 ; Ulmer, 1963 ; Nagel, 1965 ; Segal, 1984 ; Gardner, 1984), research on the subject has accelerated in recent decades. This phenomenon can be explained by the considerable increase in computing power, accompanied by major advances in the field of machine learning. 
## II.1) Synthesis of Approach and Model Types 
Several applications aimed at reducing the time spent by experts or novices on tedious tasks are being considered. In the article How Does NLP Benefit Legal System: A Summary of Legal Artificial Intelligence (Zhong, 2020), the authors suggest a framework for classifying approaches in the domain. They propose two main families of methods to group the various research efforts in the field. The first relies on symbol-based methods. In NLP, this methodology relies on the analysis of explicit rules and formal representations to understand and analyze language. The second relies on embedding models, which are dense, continuous representations of words or phrases in the form of vectors. Here, words or phrases are treated as discrete variables. Frequently linked to machine learning, this methodology yields remarkable results. However, unlike symbol-based methods, the interpretation of these models is complex or even unfeasible. While this categorization provides an initial conceptual framework, we propose adding another methodological approach: the probabilistic approach. This quantitative method analyzes texts by studying the frequency and probabilities of words, phrases, or other units extracted from texts. A variety of techniques belong to this category, such as TF-IDF (term frequency-inverse document frequency), which uses term frequency within a corpus to extract or retrieve information, or perform automatic summarization tasks (Pand, 2019 ; Basu, 2024). Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA), or Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) can also be highlighted, which, despite different mathematical constraints, offer various ways to model the topics of a text  (Didwania, 2024 ; Bermylle, 2022 ; Budahazy, 2021). We propose this classification because numerous papers continue to be published using these statistical methods. These approaches withstand the advent of machine learning thanks to their reasonable computational costs and easier interpretability. While this topology provides an analytical framework, it is important to emphasize that these categories are not mutually exclusive. Indeed, several papers combine embedding models with symbol-based or statistical approaches; sometimes all three are intertwined (Wehnert, 2021 ; Basu, 2024). 
## II.2) Current State of the Art and Critique 
Class 1: Existing Applications Useful to the Professional Community This category encompasses several tasks that research has already explored, leading to several application cases that have proven themselves over time. Legal Document Creation or Analysis: A large part of a jurist's work involves producing official documents or summaries. Research has therefore addressed this issue using a blend of statistical and embedding approaches (Basu, 2024; Devaraj, 2023). It is important to highlight that with new general embedding-based language models like GPT-4, we observe suitable performance in information extraction to generate coherent summaries (Deroy, 2024; Greco, 2024). Semantic Search Tools: Today, these tools are regularly used by the community in legal search engines, for example. Indeed, drafting legal arguments and pleadings relies on precedents. Finding judgments and citations is therefore an important task for legal teams. Research offers several approaches to assist professionals, whether through topic modeling in documents (Didwania, 2024; Bermylle, 2022; Budahazy, 2021) or citation extraction in prior decisions (Huang, 2021; Tang, 2021). These applications meet concrete needs of the justice system. Legal Language Simplification: Tools that seek to popularize legal language are important for documentalists and lawyers. Law is composed of specific vocabulary and jargon that require deep knowledge of the subjects covered. While knowledge graphs are used in many domains, no work exists for this purpose in law (Zhong, 2021). Within this framework, we find automatic summarization tools. Here, the use case differs because the objective is to simplify the analysis of a judgment or a contract, for instance (Huang et al., 2021; Tang and Clematide, 2021). Other chatbot or Q&A applications are also envisioned, allowing concepts to be clarified quickly (Khazaeli, 2021). This class also includes applications inspired by symbolbased methods, such as entity detection tasks (Akbik, 2019; Kuru, 2016), event temporality extraction (Nguyen, 2018; 2016), or relation extraction (Zeng, 2015; Christopoulou, 2018). These tools help users better grasp complex document sets, both for the community of practice and for novices in the field. Class 2: Unused or Impractical Research Applications The second class of applications groups several papers that find no echo within the legal practice community (Devaraj, 2023). The major reasons advanced for this lack of impact are model inexplicability, lack of transparency regarding training data, and the absence of community need for the tasks solved by these applications. The primary application affected by these flaws is the prediction of judgment outcomes. Indeed, lawyers or judges often do not seek to predict the outcome of a judgment; instead, they look for arguments to steer a judgment toward one set of facts over another. Regarding training data, when researchers have access to it, questions arise regarding potential biases. Ethnic, social, and cultural elements can exhibit collinearity with judgment outcomes. Thus, using a machine affected by such bias poses a danger to justice. Finally, trust in the justice system is a crucial element of our democracies. Consequently, "black box" models can be perceived by the general public as arbitrary decision-making methods, which damages trust in our institutions. However, while these applications lack proven utility in legal practice, their experimental aspect remains important for the scientific community. These tasks represent significant challenges that can advance NLP research (Chalkidis, 2022; Guha, 2023). Beyond this, while certain applications have limited utility for jurists, they can provide interesting perspectives on the domain, such as detecting bias in language (Rice, 2019). Class 3: Under-explored or Unexploited Applications The third and final category concerns applications that have not yet received sufficient attention. These use cases are inspired by a Harvard Law School study that interviewed lawyers from various countries, asking them which NLP applications would be useful to them. This study revealed several needs of legal practitioners that can be explored by current research, highlighting three application types that are underutilized or unexploited in the landscape of legal tools: Argumentation and Contextual Analysis: Argumentation is central to pleading. Therefore, it is relevant to focus on tools that identify the most pertinent arguments for a plea, incorporating variables such as jury composition, the presiding judge, or the nature of the case. While NLP studies on contextual argumentation have been proposed, no work has been adapted to the law. While this path represents a development avenue for future work, our study does not address this specific case. Confidential Information Retrieval (RAG): Law firms maintain large databases of past documents. In these private databases, information retrieval to generate new documents is an unassisted task frequently performed by lawyers themselves. The challenge here is to find relevant documents while maintaining the confidentiality of sensitive information that must not be disclosed to third parties or the public. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a method that seems suitable, but requires adjustments to comply with the legal framework. Advanced Jurisprudence and Citation Search: To prepare cases, lawyers must identify precedents and jurisprudence to support their pleadings. The challenge lies in the vast amount of available jurisprudence, which requires advanced search capabilities to build the most relevant arguments. A good decision is one that is cited regularly, corresponds as closely as possible to the case at hand, and has not been overturned by a higher court. Related to this is finding precise passages within jurisprudence to narrow down the search sample for professionals. The authors highlight prior work on legal passage prediction combined with automatic summarization as avenues for this task. In a 2021 article, Robert Mahari (Mahari, 2021) proposed fine-tuning the pretrained model proposed by Chalkidis in Legal-BERT (Chalkidis et al., 2020) to address the task of predicting citations for important texts. Using annotated data, they succeeded in identifying these passages based on their relevance. In a subsequent 2023 article, Robert Mahari (Mahari, 2023) proposed combining this approach with automatic summarization tools to simplify lawyers' research. Research has proposed automatic summarization algorithms evaluated using the ROUGE metric applied to U.S. law (Bauer, 2023). However, these approaches have never been combined to propose an application that accelerates argument research. Despite the promising results offered by these methods, the authors themselves recognize the ethical limits of these supervised approaches. Indeed, a model like BERT makes it impossible to understand the reasoning behind citation choices. Furthermore, training data introduces several biases, notably resting on past court opinions that ignore sociological and ideological factors. Thus, it could propagate past opinions without challenging the posture of judges or society on certain subjects. Moreover, this conservatism would not encourage lawyers to raise new legal questions before the courts—an important role of justice in our society. Finally, lawyers need certainty when presenting arguments before the courts. Consequently, not understanding the decision-making process generates distrust among lawyers toward these tools. Trust is essential for using legal tools because major decisions are at stake for the parties involved. Our Proposed Approach Our work focuses on jurisprudence search tools in Quebec labor law for occupational disease cases. While current research strongly focuses on supervised approaches, we wish to affirm the importance of unsupervised approaches, particularly regarding the neutralization of biases linked to data labeling. During our literature review, we noticed that using LDA to model topics in judgments is effective (Celard et al., 2024; Didwania, 2024). However, this model focuses solely on the semantic analysis of corpora. To complement the corpus study, we propose adding a graph analysis based on the citation reference network of jurisprudence and statutes present within the corpus. Combining these two methods allows us to propose a novel approach to the legal document matching task in Quebec, making it easier to retrieve similar texts within the corpus. During our review, we found little work addressing graph analysis for legal corpora. Despite this, some research proposes ingenious graph methods to reveal information in legal settings: Qiqi Wang et al. (2022) propose examining relationships between individuals in legal cases to simplify jurist analysis. To model this network, they compare classification models from machine learning, deep learning, and pretrained models like BERT. This extraction isolates facts, subjects, causal links, and logical elements, leading to graphs modeling interactions between entities and actions, entities and key concepts, and legal elements. Paul Shounak et al. (2022) focus on identifying the legal status of legal documents using a citation and reference graph network supported by text structure. Using Indian criminal law judgments associated with a graph of the Criminal Code's structure, they propose a hierarchical structure of the code (acts, chapters, articles, subjects, sections, and facts). Using judgments, they isolate facts using a Hierarchical Attention Network (HAN) encoder (Yang et al., 2016), which integrates text hierarchical structure and pays attention to words and phrases. The combination of structural and network analysis is achieved via a scoring function yielding attribute, structural, and alignment scores. This experiment revealed better performance scores than competing methods like LegalBERT. Yanran Tang et al. (2023) similarly seek to improve legal text matching using a Graph Neural Network (GNN). Using Canadian Federal Court data, they apply a GNN to texts to extract the argumentation structure of cases. While these methods examine the internal structure of jurisprudence, they do not examine the reference structure between texts and statutes. However, notably in scientific publishing, several papers propose using citation networks for tasks such as recommending related articles (Hanwen et al., 2019; Masaki, 2019). We propose using a similar approach here for Quebec law. Our methodology and the particularities of our data will be detailed in the next section.
