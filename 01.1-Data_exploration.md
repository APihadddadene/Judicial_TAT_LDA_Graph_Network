# IV.1) Data Exploration
To better understand our corpus, we propose an exploratory analysis of its data. In accordance with the tasks we wish to perform, we examine semantic and graph-structure data. This data is primarily descriptive, but it allows us to derive intuitions that our analyses will verify. Beyond that, it reveals potential extrema to be studied within the corpus as well as the challenges to be taken into account when implementing our models.
## IV.1.a) Quantitative exploration of semantic data
As specified in the chapter on data collection, we have 5,403 texts in .txt format. To be rigorous, we checked using a Python script whether there were any duplicates in our data. As a result, we found one text identical to another and removed it, which reduces our corpus to a count of 5,402 texts. In order to better grasp the content of the corpus, we proceed with an exploration of the latter via statistical analyses. To do this, we looked at the number of characters, the number of words, and the number of sentences present in the texts. While Python offers native features for counting characters, words and sentences require a prior operation: tokenization. Tokenization consists of dividing a text into smaller units, called tokens, which are generally words or sentences. For this, we use the spacy library, which provides a tokenizer for words and sentences. We segment our texts into words and sentences in order to perform a statistical analysis of our corpus. The first observation we can make about this analysis concerns the length of the texts. Indeed, we had inferred that legal texts were relatively long because they contain arguments from judges to support their decisions. We can see that our corpus is composed of texts ranging between 987 and 126,056 words, which confirms this intuition. To support our analysis, we produced histograms of the distribution of texts according to their number of characters, words, and sentences, available in Appendices 1 to 3. These appendices graphically indicate a right-skewed distribution of our corpus's length. In other words, a vast majority of the texts have fewer than 10,000 words; more specifically, 75% of our corpus comprises fewer than 9,554 words. Furthermore, we can also observe a group of judgments that are shorter than the others—referring here to fewer than 1,600 words. Part of these texts consists of administrative reviews by the tribunal confirming or dismissing a decision without truly developing arguments. The succinct nature of certain decisions can also be explained by judges referring cases to other instances. Indeed, in certain files, the requests do not fall under the jurisdiction granted by law to the TAT. In this situation, the judge briefly justifies the nature of the referral without delving deeper into the case. We can also see a few very long judgments, as shown by our maximum of 126,056 words. However, these texts are rare, as our histograms demonstrate. In general, these are complex cases that deal with multiple subjects or accident files within a single judgment. In such instances, the judge must review two or three related cases and elaborate reasoning for each case before presenting their decision, which increases the length of the judgment. From this initial analysis, we note that the dimensionality of our data sample is large, since each unique word is grouped as a variable for the LDA model. Despite grouping the tokens to form the variables, we can reasonably infer that, without data manipulation, the number of variables remains too vast for our model. This high dimensionality is a recurring issue in NLP work, leading to a loss of statistical signal that affects the performance of the models used.

To deepen our understanding of the corpus, we analyzed the forty most frequent words in our corpus, as presented in Appendices 5 and 6. We propose two analyses: one without any data manipulation and one with cleaning. The necessity of cleaning proved essential, as Appendix 5 shows us that the most frequent terms are grammatical words and punctuation elements. This highlights another characteristic element of textual data: information asymmetry. While this characteristic is never named as such in publications dealing with NLP, it is induced by the nature of word distribution within a text. In Western languages, the distribution frequently follows George K. Zipf’s postulate (Moreno et al., 2019), which indicates that the frequency of a word in a corpus is inversely proportional to its rank, namely:

If conditions have been added over time to this law, we can see that the texts tend to follow this law. Beyond the distribution of words within the text or the corpus, we can observe that, generally, grammatical words are the most frequent (e.g., le, la, les, ce, etc.). However, these elements of language do not carry meaning outside the context of the sentence in which they are found. For example, the word "bicycle" has a clear meaning for all speakers who know it. It therefore carries strong information compared to the word "that" (cela), which does not evoke anything distinct depending on the context. This information asymmetry must lead us to pay particular attention to how we reduce dimensionality. We must ensure that we do not remove variables that are highly informative for our models, even if they are less frequent in our texts. Appendix 6 shows us the effect of cleaning on the informativeness derived from the most frequent words after a brief cleaning of our tokens. Size reduction was performed by removing punctuation and layout elements, such as \n (for a line break) and /s (for additional spaces). We also used the NLTK package, which contains a list of frequently removed grammatical words in French, known as stop-words (Appendix 4). While a few grammatical words are kept, these manipulations already allow us to extract more information from our corpus. We can see three lexicon categories emerging:

Work-related words: worker (travailleur/se), employer, employment, etc.

Health-related words: doctor, lesion, medical, etc.

Tribunal jargon words: file, administrative, event

We can note a word like gauche (left), which relates to the side of the lesion or injury, but also droit, which is ambiguous in our case as it can refer either to law (droit) or to the right side. We also proposed an analysis of bigrams and trigrams, which are sequences of the two or three most frequent words in the texts. We propose a modeling of the most frequent ones in Appendices 7, 8, 9, and 10. Through this exploration, we notice that terms like l5 s1 are frequently present together. This element seems suspicious to us, and we took a closer look at the texts containing these bigrams. Through this verification, we found that these were medical names for vertebrae, and that C1, C2, ..., C7 refer to the cervical vertebrae. Thus, we take these factors into account to normalize these elements, in order to avoid the deletion of these important words during our cleaning steps. Regarding the trigram analysis, we do not extract any major information that requires special attention.

Our analyses also indicate that while dimensionality is high, the matrices are sparse. Indeed, several terms will be missing in most texts of the set, or will only be present in a single one. This characteristic can complicate the interpretation and visualization of the data as well as the operation of our models.

In summary, this exploration has shown us several elements. First, we outlined three NLP challenges that we face for our research:

High dimensionality

Sparse matrices

Information asymmetry

These challenges are indications that multiple data manipulations are necessary to optimize the operation of our models and the interpretability of the results. Indeed, since LDA forms word vectors, statistical noise is likely to impair the legibility of the topics formed by the algorithm as well as the performance and robustness of topic modeling. Dimensionality reduction must take into account the particular characteristics of textual data. We detail these cleanings in our section on LDA analyses.

## IV.1.b) Graph Data Exploration
Our graph consists of 856 laws, regulations, and articles of laws and regulations, as well as 11,187 jurisprudential references linked to our corpus of 5,403 texts. This notable difference between jurisprudential references and laws is explained by the fact that judges often cite the article(s) of law at the beginning of the judgment or decision, but the number and quality of the precedents invoked to justify the tribunal's interpretation of the law are an important factor for the robustness of the judgment against potential administrative appeals. Indeed, as explained in our chapter on the case of Quebec labor law, the court's logic is paramount to ensuring a fair and equitable judgment. Thus, summoning prior decisions is a reliable way to ensure this, hence the unbalanced nature between the two types of nodes in our graph.

In addition, we looked at descriptive statistics on all citations, jurisprudence citations, and law citations in the corpus. We analyze the distribution of citations in the latter, presented in Table 2 and Appendices 11 to 13.

Our analysis reveals that the texts contain between 0 and 105 citations. Looking closer at our data, we find 73 texts without citations. We read these texts and, from this analysis, we note two categories of judgments: tribunal corrections and medical opinions. Tribunal corrections are judgments that correct formal elements of prior decisions or compel a party to execute a tribunal order. The latter focus more on form than substance, hence the non-citation of articles of law or jurisprudence within them. Medical opinions are judgments focusing on counter-expert assessments that verify the validity of a medical evaluation proposed by an initial physician. Consequently, these decisions do not rely on legal opinions, but rather on medical documentation and medical publications. Beyond that, we notice that half of our corpus contains between 0 and 5 citations, and three-quarters of it contain fewer than 15. Looking at the descriptive statistics and our histogram, we find that the distribution is left-skewed, which makes sense because few texts tend to cite a large number of references. However, if we look at the texts between the third quartile and the maximum, we see that some judgments have an extensive list of citations. Looking at some of these texts, notably the one with the most citations, we note that they are long texts. For example, QCTAT2899, which contains 105 citations, is a 36-page text containing debates on certain arguments. We can also highlight paragraphs with many citations, which may be linked to contentious arguments leading the court to support its decision with a large number of references. We put forward the hypothesis here that texts with a large number of citations are important texts because they contain discussions requiring more citations to support the judge's arguments. If our hypothesis is verified, this implies that these texts are important for labor law, as they involve debates requiring the support of numerous sources. We strive to verify this in our analyses and results. In addition to this, we also have the distribution of texts according to the number of jurisprudence citations and law citations separately. We observe an imbalance between the two classes when looking at the third quartile in both situations. Indeed, three-quarters of the texts cite fewer than 10 pieces of jurisprudence and fewer than 5 laws. We also see that the maximum number of judicial precedent citations is 91 compared to 31 for laws. Looking graphically at the distributions in Appendices 9 and 10, we notice that few texts in the corpus cite no laws at all. The average number of law citations is also significantly lower than the average number of precedent citations. This can be explained by the fact that the law is the foundational element for introducing a case to the tribunal. In other words, the TAT uses the law as the basis for argumentation, and generally a few articles of law are sufficient to conduct the proceedings, which explains why less than half of our corpus relies on fewer than 4 laws. On the other hand, to understand how to apply and interpret the law, prior decisions are essential, as explained in our chapter on Quebec administrative labor law. Thus, it seems natural and logical that a greater number of jurisprudences are summoned to support one argument or another. In this sense, Appendix 9 shows us that the distribution of texts according to the number of jurisprudences they cite follows the trend presented for all citations in Appendix 8. Even though the overall number of these citations is greater than that of the laws, we still see that a large majority of texts cite only between 0 and 5 jurisprudences. In certain situations, the court probably needs few references because the cases presented are solidified by law, and the tribunals do not require a large number of arguments to support the decision. All of this brings us to another point, which is the quality of the references. Indeed, the cases that cite may rely on references commonly recognized as being of higher quality, which are self-sufficient. This element implies that certain jurisprudences are quality elements that recur in judgments dealing with similar cases, and therefore have a strong presence in the corpus. This second hypothesis, complementary to the first, implies that judgments with few citations rely on jurisprudential citations used repeatedly, which testifies to their quality.

Our exploratory analysis of the graph data reveals that the citation network, although vast, contains key elements to be studied in depth. We have posed two hypotheses here:

The large quantity of citations a text contains reveals the importance of the latter for Quebec labor law.

The small quantity of citations a text contains indicates the quality of those citations.

The verification of these hypotheses is addressed in our results, which focus on similarity indices and distances held by our corpus. These similarity indices are to be analyzed between the texts of the corpus, but also between the various references it contains. The verification of these hypotheses allows us to highlight important information, notably regarding case matching if they prove valid.
